**FOUNDATIONAL CV / 3D-GEOMETRY KNOWLEDGE (general principles — apply as needed):**

These are the physics and statistics of turning pixels into geometry. They are background facts, not a recipe: read each tool's description for what it returns, then compose the primitives the question in front of you actually needs.

*Pinhole camera & projection.*
- A pinhole camera maps a 3D point to a pixel by `x_pixel ∝ K · [R | t] · X_world`, where the intrinsics `K` (focal length, principal point) encode the lens and the extrinsics `[R | t]` (equivalently the cam2world pose) encode where the camera sits and looks. Projection is many-to-one: every 3D point on the ray through a pixel lands on that same pixel.
- Because projection collapses a ray to a point, a single 2D pixel does NOT determine a 3D point — it determines only a direction. You need depth (distance along the ray) to recover the 3D location: `X_cam = depth · K⁻¹ · [u, v, 1]`, then `X_world = cam2world · X_cam`. This is back-projection, and it is why every 2D detection must be paired with depth before it means anything metric.
- A camera-to-world pose is a rigid transform: its rotation columns are the camera axes expressed in world coordinates and its translation column is the camera's world position. The camera-forward axis is one rotation column; project it onto the floor plane and renormalize to get a heading. Cross products of floor-plane direction vectors give signed left/right relations: in this Z-up world, `cross(forward_xy, target_xy)` with a positive Z (vertical) component means the target is to the LEFT of forward, negative means RIGHT. The signed turn angle between two headings is `atan2(cross_z, dot)`; its sign is left(+)/right(−) and a magnitude near 180° is a near-reversal (a turn back the way you came). Compute these in code rather than by eye.

*Depth, metric scale & gravity.*
- Monocular depth from a single image is ambiguous up to an unknown scale: a small near object and a large far object can produce identical pixels. Without metric grounding you only recover relative depth, so any real-world length, area, or distance requires a metrically-scaled reconstruction — a per-pixel `pts3d_world` field already lifts pixels into a consistent metric world frame, so prefer reading 3D points from it over re-deriving them.
- Gravity/floor alignment matters: when the world frame is aligned so that one axis points along gravity (here Z-up, floor on the XY plane), "height", "horizontal footprint", and "which things are stacked" become simple coordinate comparisons instead of arbitrary-plane fits. In a gravity-aligned cloud the floor is the lowest horizontal slab — near the low percentile of Z, not the raw minimum, which is dragged down by sub-floor depth noise. Reason about horizontal layout in XY and vertical structure in Z separately.

*Multi-view geometry & data association.*
- The same physical object is seen in many frames, producing one raw detection per visible frame. These are redundant observations of ONE entity, not many entities. Treating each frame's detection as a distinct object (naive concatenation) double-counts; the detections must first be associated to physical identity.
- Association links detections that are the same object across frames, by spatial proximity (3D distance between lifted centroids, 3D-IoU of lifted point sets, or voxel-occupancy overlap), 2D-IoU/tracking within a sequence, or appearance/embedding similarity. A robust merge unions any two detections whose inlier point sets overlap substantially in a shared voxel grid (joined transitively, e.g. via union-find); genuinely distinct objects occupy disjoint voxels and stay separate. Trim boundary outliers from each detection BEFORE associating, or a few stray edge points bridge separate objects.
- Multiple viewpoints are an asset: parallax (the apparent shift of an object against its background as the camera moves) is what makes depth recoverable and lets the same point be triangulated from several rays. More independent views reduce variance — but only after correct association; more views of a mis-associated set just amplify the error.

*Point-cloud robustness.*
- Depth bleed at object/background boundaries is the dominant point-cloud error: pixels on a silhouette edge interpolate between the near object surface and the far background (wall, floor, further furniture). Including them inflates extents outward AND biases centroids toward the background. The boundary, not the interior, is where the noise lives.
- Defend against this before any geometric measurement: prefer per-axis percentile bounds (e.g. 5th–95th) over raw min/max, which are pure outlier statistics; voxelize to merge redundant points and regularize density; and trim inliers (drop the extreme-percentile tail per axis) before computing centroids, extents, or distances. A tight silhouette (mask) bleeds far less than a containing rectangle (bbox), and eroding a mask a few pixels strips the bleed ring at the cost of a little real surface — erode more when you only need a clean center, less when you need a faithful perimeter.
- The closest-surface distance between two point clouds is NOT the gap between their bounding boxes: AABBs collapse to zero separation whenever they overlap, even when the actual surfaces are apart (e.g. a chair tucked under a table). True minimum separation is a nearest-neighbour query between the two raw inlier point sets, not a box comparison.

*Segmentation & detection properties.*
- A bounding box is the smallest axis-aligned rectangle containing an object; a segmentation mask is its per-pixel silhouette. The box always includes background in its corners (more bleed when lifted to 3D); the mask follows the object outline and is far cleaner — prefer masks for anything metric.
- Masks carry their own failure modes: boundary pixels are contaminated (erode before lifting); a mask defined on the image grid must be resampled to the depth/point grid before it can index 3D points; and an object cut off at the frame edge (truncation) yields a mask biased toward its visible side — its centroid and extent are unreliable, so down-weight or drop edge-truncated observations.
- Detectors and trackers fail in two opposite directions. A tracker can DRIFT — lose an object and re-acquire it under a fresh identity, minting phantom instances (over-count) — or MISS an instance entirely, or fail to show all instances in any single frame (under-count). A genuine object persists across many frames; a drift-spawned phantom appears in only one or two, so persistence across frames is the signal that separates real instances from artifacts. Per-frame instance ids are generally not comparable across frames unless a tracker explicitly maintains identity.

*Aggregation & estimator hygiene.*
- Aggregate across observations with robust statistics: a median over per-frame measurements rejects the occasional bad frame; a single measurement should be distrusted, especially if it sits far from the others or rests on a truncated/low-coverage detection. Reduce per-frame results to one robust value, do not concatenate everything and measure the union (one bad frame poisons the whole pool). Before merging the same object's point clouds across frames, compute each frame's centroid and the max pairwise spread: if per-frame centroids for one object disagree by more than ~0.5 m, the combined cloud is corrupted by camera-pose error (not measurement variance) — do not merge it for a closest-point query; measure from the single most internally-consistent frame instead. When comparing two objects grounded in different frames, lift a shared salient point (e.g. one object's centroid) from both frames and confirm the world coordinates agree within ~0.2 m before trusting the cross-frame distance; large frame-index gaps accumulate pose error proportional to the gap.
- Two different estimators of the same quantity often carry opposite biases — one systematically under-shoots, the other over-shoots — so they bracket the truth from both sides; when they agree, trust the value, and when they diverge, the gap itself bounds the error and flags which way each is wrong. A near-zero distance between two supposedly distinct objects, or a count that contradicts the premise of the question, is a signal of a detection/association failure, not a valid measurement.

*Determinism discipline.*
- Do non-trivial numeric and geometric computation in code, not as in-context arithmetic. Cross-frame association by IoU, clustering / union-find dedup, percentile aggregation over many points, vector geometry on more than a few inputs, KD-tree nearest-neighbour queries, and score-sorting with ties are all error-prone when done mentally and silently wrong when they are. Compute them in the sandbox, print the result, and reason about that — the verifier cannot catch an in-context arithmetic slip, but executed code is reproducible and inspectable.
- When you issue several tool calls at once and the results are unlabeled lists (frame indices, instances), do NOT assume responses arrive in call order: re-associate each response to the label it asked for before using any value, or re-issue the ambiguous calls sequentially. When several returned fields measure the same quantity at different noise levels, commit from the most-filtered field that has non-zero support (persistence-filtered over raw union over single-frame peak), not whichever appeared last in context.
