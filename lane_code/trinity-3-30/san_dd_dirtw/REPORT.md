# Toward direction on the ReVSI-style rooms (R0-dev): why 69 items stay wrong (lane san_dd_dirtw, 2026-11-19)

The 69 always-wrong toward items are errors in the model's spatial reading of the frames, and the one-line trace only reports them. The answer copies the trace's stated relation on 376 of 376 toward items at every step, the relation line rests on nothing, and three checkpoints under three trace forms state the same wrong option on 66 of the 69. No geometric, lexical or object factor explains the failures. Object size, name rarity, role exchange and nearness to a boundary (by step 544's own angle) each leave accuracy nearly flat, or separate wrong items from right ones only weakly. The one factor that does carry signal is the room: rooms with always-wrong toward items also fail away direction, relative distance, appearance order and counting, and side errors cluster by room. All sets are dev sets; prices are proxies.

## 1. Answers

**Q1. Classification of the 115 wrong items (step 200).** I read all 115 beside steps 544 and R1-100, and 60 right items (seeded sample) the same way. The per-item table is `work/tw_items.tsv` (objects, truth, the option each step states, step 544's angle, stability, class). My rule assigns one geometric class per item, in this order:
- (d) gross: the answer is the opposite quadrant.
- (a) boundary: step 544's angle lies within 25 degrees of the line that separates the truth's region from the answer's. The lines are 0 and 180 degrees for easy; 0 degrees (left against right) or ±135 (side against back) for medium; 0, 180 or ±90 for hard.
- (b) mirrored: the side is flipped, front and back are kept, and the item is not a boundary case.
- (c) front-back: front and back are flipped with the side kept, or a medium side and back are exchanged, and the item is not a boundary case.
- (e) unstable is a second axis: an item wrong at step 200 but right at step 544 or step 100.
- (f) label or instance is a flag. No item can be shown to carry a label error (Q3, Q5).

Counts: (a) 49, (b) 43, (c) 18, (d) 5. The always-wrong 69 hold 25, 27, 12 and 5 of them, and (e) holds the other 46. Prices are in Table 1. The 69 always-wrong items cost 1.31 ReVSI points (17 easy, 28 hard); the 2.15 belongs to all 74 easy and hard errors (section 4).

Boundary nearness separates wrong items from right ones only weakly. Step 544's angle lies within 25 degrees of a boundary of the truth's region on 50 of 115 wrong items and on 95 of 261 right items (43% against 36%; hard 24 of 46 against 43 of 75). The 60-item right sample gives 22 of 60. The reason is that step 544 draws its angle from a small vocabulary: 66 distinct values, and 21, 17, 107, 117, 27, 16 and 20 degrees cover 198 of 376 items. The smallest values sit 15 to 25 degrees from the 0-degree line, so "boundary" here means the model placed the target "slightly" to a side. It says nothing about the true angle. Step 544 is right on 59%, 58% and 63% of easy, medium and hard items whose angle is at most 25 degrees, and on 87%, 76% and 89% at 26 to 60 degrees. Classes (b) and (d) are confident errors: of the 27 always-wrong mirrored items, 22 carry a step-544 angle 43 to 117 degrees away from the separating line, and 4 of the 5 gross items were already in the opposite quadrant at step 544.

**Q2. Exchanged roles.** The side of C, seen from A facing B, is the orientation of triangle ABC. Exchanging any two roles reverses that orientation, so all three exchanges flip the side. On easy items they all predict the only wrong option, which makes them uninformative there. Front and back behave differently. Exchanging the faced object and the target negates the bearing, so it keeps front, back and the medium back band. Exchanging the observer with either the faced object or the target turns a back target into a front one, and a front target can then fall either way. The determined predictions are therefore these. On medium, the faced-target exchange predicts a left-right swap and no error on a back truth, while the observer exchanges allow every wrong answer. On hard, the faced-target exchange predicts one option, and the observer exchanges predict the opposite quadrant for back truths. Against a uniform choice among wrong options, the wrong answers match the faced-target exchange more often than chance (hard 25 of 46, chance 15.3, p = 0.003). The model's own answer frequencies predict almost the same share, however: 22.6 on hard, 21.4 against 21 on front truths, and 25.8 against 26 on medium side truths. No exchange therefore explains the errors beyond the model's preference for front and for a side. Back-truth hard errors go to the opposite quadrant on 4 of 19 (8.9 expected) and to the front with the side kept on 11 of 19, which no exchange produces (Table 2). Order: 502 of the 528 parsable rows in a 600-row primary-key sample of toward rows name observer, faced object and target in that order; 26 name the faced object first ("With the sofa ahead of me as I stand by the refrigerator, which direction describes the radiator", row 57511). R0-dev names them in that order on 376 of 376, all from one template. dev-S1 uses paraphrases (95 in that order and 8 faced-first among the 103 my parser read).

**Q3. Several instances.** The question cannot be answered on R0-dev. Its counting items count only classes with two or more instances (truth 2 to 6+ on 287 of 287 single-category items), and a toward item names a counted class on 2 of 376 items (1 wrong, 1 right). On dev-S1, 6 of 52 wrong items and 5 of 388 right items name a class counted at 2 or more. Without object positions I cannot tell whether another instance explains an answer: the trace names a class, never an instance, and no line locates it. Nested names (one role's name contains another's: "storage cabinet" faced, "cabinet" target in `a2b5a9`) occur on 4 of 115 wrong and 8 of 261 right items, so they show no excess.

**Q4. What kind of object.** Neither size nor rarity explains the failures (Table 3). On R0-dev, items whose smallest object is under 30 cm score 66% (121 items), 30 to 100 cm score 69% (93) and over 100 cm score 82% (11). Sizes come from the room's size items for 217 object mentions and from the median of the 9,778 training size rows for 678. In the 2,000-row direction sample, the rarest name's band is non-monotone: 67% (293 items, name absent), 85% (47) and 67% (36). Against all 93,370 store v7 direction questions (1,212 names) the curve is flat: 69.9% (156 items, a name never used), 69.0% (171, 1 to 99 rows) and 69.4% (49, at least 100 rows). On dev-S1, items whose names all appear in at least 100 rows score 88.5% (407). R0-dev items with fully familiar names therefore still trail dev-S1 by 19 points.

**Q5. Rooms.** The 69 always-wrong items are spread out. They fall in 47 of 97 rooms (29 rooms with one, 14 with two, 4 with three), and the five worst rooms hold 14 of 69 against 12.7 under random placement (p = 0.28). The rooms still matter. Rooms with two or more always-wrong items score 51.5% on away (33 items) against 67.6% (111) in rooms with none, 62.7% against 73.5% on relative distance, 52.0% against 68.5% on appearance order, and 43.2% against 57.5% on counting. Per room, toward accuracy correlates with away (r = 0.33), relative distance (0.22) and appearance order (0.19). Side errors cluster by room: 9 of 79 rooms get every side judgement but one wrong, against 4.8 under shuffling (p = 0.021; at step 544, 12 against 6.1). No room gets every side wrong, so no room is wholly mirrored, whether in its labels or in the model's view. I read every direction item of the four rooms with three always-wrong items; each has six items. The step-200 answers fit one 2-D layout in 3 of the 4 (223194 fits 5 of 6). Random answer sets fit 8 of 8 draws in three of these rooms, though, and 704 of 776 draws over all rooms. With six items per room, "one consistent but wrong layout" therefore cannot be told from noise. In three rooms a single object recurs in the wrong items: the eraser in 21c5ab (3 of 3 items that name it are wrong), the metal tray in af2e82 (3 of 3) and the power strip in 223194 (3 of 4). Across all rooms, sharing an object raises the chance that a second item is also wrong only from 32% to 41%, and objects whose items are all wrong are no more common than under a within-room shuffle (44 against 45.5, p = 0.68).

**Q6. Why R0-dev and not dev-S1.** Timing, back-truth share and wording are matched; the room source and the vocabulary differ (Table 4). I read 40 hard toward items of each set side by side; they score 20 of 40 (R0-dev) and 33 of 40 (dev-S1). Their videos are equally long (median 136 against 132 s), their sampled frames are equally spaced (4.4 against 4.3 s), they carry 8 and 9 back truths, and step 544's angle falls near a boundary on 24 and 19 items. Every R0-dev item runs at 60 fps, and dev-S1's 60-fps ScanNet++ items score 87.1% (233). The sources differ in three ways:
- dev-S1 items are VSI-590K items ("vsi590k_" ids), the corpus behind 43,469 training qids, and use paraphrased wording. R0-dev items come from one template, whose wording 342 of 600 sampled training toward rows share.
- dev-S1 rooms carry the dataset tags of every training row: d1 (ScanNet, 24 fps), d2 (ScanNet++, 60 fps) and d3 (ARKitScenes, 10 fps). The tag-to-dataset mapping comes from the rows' license field. R0-dev rooms carry an untagged 24-hex id.
- R0-dev toward items name an object absent from the 2,000 sampled rows on 293 of 376 items; dev-S1 items do so on 24 of 440.

dev-S1 rooms are themselves held out. None of the 1,339 dev-S1 ids is a training qid, and none of dev-S1's 27 ScanNet++ rooms appears among the 560 ScanNet++ scenes of the training size rows. Training scene ids are opaque ("v" plus 12 hex, 1,979 scenes over the 93,370 direction rows), so I cannot test R0-dev rooms against them. Q4 rules out vocabulary and the matched timing rules out time. By elimination, which is an inference and not a measurement, what remains is the rooms themselves or the way R0-dev computes its labels. The cross-type room failures of Q5 favour the rooms. But R0-dev's relative distance trails dev-S1 by only 6 points (67.4 against 73.3, record section 2) while toward direction trails by 18, so part of the gap is specific to direction, and label geometry stays a candidate (section 5). Which source the rooms come from is not established (guess: ScanNet++-like 60 fps captures with a long-tail label set).

**Q7. An evidence-bearing trace.** The ruling allows Locate lines (an object, one frame from the row's source, optionally a coarse image region), Relate lines in words, Step lines and, for program types only, Call lines. It forbids any angle or coordinate that no response printed, and it keeps direction direct. The three candidate forms fare as follows; the verbatim examples are in section 2.
- (1) Locate lines before the relation ("Seen: ... in frame k"). The ruling allows them, and the "Seen:" pools carry them (43,469 rows). The source must supply a best-view or first-seen frame per object; the w1g family (49,901 rows) does not, and no row I read carries an image region. Expected effect on the 115 is a few items at most. At step 200 the model already opens 45 of 376 toward traces with "Seen:" and gets 38 of 45 right, but those items were already easier for step 544 (78% against 66%) and step 100 (84% against 70%) under other forms. Its numbers are also not frame indices: 356 of 599 cited numbers exceed 32, and all but 5 of those lie within the video's length in seconds. They sit no closer to the sampled frames' timestamps than random times do (median 0.33 against 0.24 frame spacings), so they are not those frames' times either.
- (2) A turn read off the camera's motion between the objects' frames. The grammar has no such line. A worded turn between two cited frames, with no number other than the frame indices, would be a Relate line, and I take it to need a ruling. The source must supply camera poses for the cited frames, but direction rows carry frame indices and timestamps only (their keys hold no pose). Expected effect: little. The nearest trained wording, the VSTI viewpoint relation, scores only 70.0 on VSTI left-right at step 200 (record, section 2), no better than R0-dev toward.
- (3) Head calls. The `<ground>` response prints each object's centre in the shared frame f1 with y vertical, so three calls fix a bearing on the x-z plane. The builder's facing rows (unadmitted) carry exactly this form. The ruling rejects it for the seal: programs lost 10.0 to 27.3 direction points against direct with deployed masks and gained nothing with ground-truth masks, and text atan2 errs on 13% to 30% of items. Only hard direction stays open, as a gated experiment (O3). The sizes these responses print look wrong (a bed of 0.55 x 0.10 x 0.22 m), so their centres are suspect too. Expected effect: unknown. The form can move hard errors only if its bearing beats R0-dev hard's 62% on R0-dev rooms, and no read has measured that.

## 4. Refuted or corrected

- The brief prices the 69 always-wrong items at "2.15 ReVSI points". The 2.15 is the price of all step-200 toward errors on the easy and hard leaves (28 easy plus 46 hard; record, section 11, "wrong relation facing the reference 2.15"). The 69 cost 1.31 points (17 easy, 28 hard); their 24 medium items carry no ReVSI weight.
- The brief's definition of (b) says that exchanging the observer and the faced object keeps front and back. It does not: that exchange turns a back target into a front one and leaves a front target undetermined. Only exchanging the faced object and the target keeps front and back while flipping the side. Without positions, "stand at B, face A" can be tested only as a side flip.
- The first-wave reports (`dir_easy`, `dir_med`, `dir_hard`): my reading agrees with their counts (28, 26 plus 15, and 46 toward errors; 25 side, 16 front-back and 5 both on hard). I extend `dir_hard`'s "lexical novelty alone shows no error increase" to all 93,370 rows: the R0-dev curve is flat (69.9, 69.0, 69.4). Over the 2,000-row sample alone the curve is non-monotone (67.2% on 293 items against 77.1% on 83), and only the full count shows that this difference is noise.

## 5. Not established, and what would settle it

- Whether any of the 115 are label errors, or fall inside the rows' 5-degree and 10-degree margins. Truths fit one layout in 97 of 97 rooms, but random answers fit in 91% of draws, so the test has no power. R0-dev object centres from the build record would settle it.
- The source of R0-dev's rooms, and whether a training split of it exists. The R0-dev build manifest would settle it.
- Whether a misplaced object explains the three top rooms (eraser, metal tray, power strip). A dev set with ten or more questions per object in a room would settle it.
- What the Seen-form numbers above 32 refer to. They fall within the video's length but not on sampled-frame times. Reading the cited frames against the video would settle it.
- How accurate the head's bearing is on R0-dev rooms (item 4).
